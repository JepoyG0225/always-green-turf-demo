Jobber & QuickBooks Automations

What happens automatically between Jobber and QuickBooks, in plain English.

1. Why these automations exist

The office runs on two systems that don't talk to each other on their own:

  • Jobber is where the work lives — quotes, jobs, schedules, invoices, and the payments customers make.
  • QuickBooks Online is where the money lives — the customer list, the invoices the accountant works from, and the payments against them.

Without automation, somebody has to retype every customer, every invoice and every payment from Jobber into QuickBooks. That is slow, and small typing mistakes turn into books that don't balance. These automations do that copying, in the background, within seconds of each step happening in Jobber.

The one-line version: the sales team works in ArcSite and Jobber exactly as they always have. QuickBooks fills itself in behind them.

2. The four systems involved

SystemWhat it's forWho touches it
ArcSiteThe drawing and the proposal the customer sees, with the pricing.Sales reps, in the field

QuickBooks OnlineCustomers, projects, invoices, payments — the accounting record.Bookkeeping

Nobody has to open QuickBooks to make any of this happen. The automations watch Jobber and write to QuickBooks on their own.

3. One customer, start to finish

Here is the whole path for a typical install, and what runs by itself at each step.

4. Proposal sent → Jobber quote

What starts it: a rep sends a proposal from ArcSite.

What it does:

  1. Reads the pricing from the ArcSite drawing — every line item, any discount, and the processing fee.
  2. Finds the customer in Jobber. If they're genuinely new, the customer is created, with the address taken from the proposal title.
  3. Matches the ArcSite rep to the Jobber user, so the quote is credited to the right salesperson. The email is used to match, because the display names often differ.
  4. Creates the quote in Jobber and attaches the proposal PDF as a note.
Worth knowing: the totals come from the line totals in ArcSite, not the unit rates. Some items — pallet deliveries, certain paver borders — are priced in tiers, where the rate shown is not what the customer is charged. Reading the wrong one used to inflate those quotes.

5. Quote signed → PDF on the quote

What starts it: the customer signs the proposal in ArcSite.

What it does: attaches the signed PDF to the matching Jobber quote, so the signed copy lives with the quote instead of in someone's email.

If the proposal is still a draft, pending, or expired, nothing happens — it only acts on an approved signature.

6. Job created → QuickBooks customer

What starts it: a quote is turned into a job in Jobber.

What it does: creates two things in QuickBooks:

  • the customer — the person, with their billing address; and
  • a project underneath them, named the same.

The project is where the invoice goes. It keeps each job's income separate, so a repeat customer's second install doesn't get mixed in with their first.

This step is the gateway. Nothing else creates the QuickBooks customer. If a job is never created in Jobber, that customer will never appear in QuickBooks — which is why a deposit now creates the job automatically rather than waiting.

7. Job closed → the invoice

What starts it: the job is closed in Jobber.

What it does:

  1. Creates the invoice in Jobber from the job's line items, carrying over the same processing-fee decision the quote used.
  2. Copies that invoice into QuickBooks, on the customer's project, using the same invoice number.
  3. Applies any deposit the customer already paid, so both systems show the same balance owing.

Jobber records a discount and the processing fee as adjustments to the whole invoice; QuickBooks has no equivalent, so each becomes its own line. That's why a QuickBooks invoice can show a "Discount" or "Processing Fee" line that isn't on the Jobber one — the totals still match to the cent.

If a job is closed twice, the second run does nothing: it checks whether there's anything left uninvoiced before creating anything.

8. Payment received → applying the money

What starts it: any payment recorded in Jobber — card, ACH, cheque or cash.

This is the one with the most judgement in it, because a payment doesn't say which invoice it belongs to. It works through the possibilities in order:

SituationWhat it does
One open QuickBooks invoice for exactly that amountApplies it. Invoice shows paid.
Only one open invoice, and the payment is smallerApplies it as a part payment and reports the balance still owing.
A deposit against a signed quoteHolds it — see Deposits below — and starts the job if there isn't one.
The customer isn't in QuickBooks at allBuilds the missing side from Jobber — customer, project and invoice — then applies the payment.
Several open invoices and no clear matchStops and asks. Slack shows Payment needs review.

The last row matters: when it isn't sure, it does nothing rather than guessing. A payment applied to the wrong invoice is far harder to unpick than one that waited for a human.

9. Deposits, explained

A deposit is paid when the customer signs — before there's any invoice to put it against. Jobber holds it on the quote, and only moves it onto an invoice when the job is invoiced. QuickBooks can't hold it anywhere at that point, because the invoice doesn't exist yet.

So a deposit follows this path:

  1. Customer pays; Slack reports Deposit received and names the quote.
  2. If nobody has turned the quote into a job yet, the job is created automatically, which creates the QuickBooks customer and project.
  3. The money stays held on the quote — this is normal, not an error.
  4. When the job is closed and invoiced, the deposit is applied to that invoice in both systems at once.
If you see "Deposit received" and nothing else for weeks, it means the job hasn't been closed yet. The money is safe on the quote in Jobber the whole time; it simply hasn't reached an invoice.

10. Why the numbers match

A QuickBooks invoice now carries the same invoice number as the Jobber invoice. Jobber invoice #2925 becomes QuickBooks invoice 2925. When a customer rings about "invoice 2925", the same number finds it in either system.

Two exceptions you might notice:

  • If that number is somehow already used in QuickBooks, the new one gets a suffix — 2925-1 — so it's still recognisable.
  • Invoices created before this change may have a different number, or none at all. The older ones have since been renumbered by hand to match.

11. What the automations refuse to do

Just as important as what they do. In each of these cases the automation stops and tells you, rather than acting on a guess:

  • It won't add a sales rep as a customer. If an ArcSite drawing has the rep's own name in the customer field, it refuses and asks for the drawing to be fixed.
  • It won't guess between similar names. "Christie Friendly" when "Christy Friendly" already exists could be a typo or a different person — creating one duplicates a real customer, assuming quotes the wrong one. It names the suspect and waits.
  • It won't apply an ambiguous payment. Several open invoices and no clean match means review.
  • It won't invoice the same job twice, or copy the same invoice into QuickBooks twice.
  • It won't pay an invoice that's already settled.

12. Watching it work

Two places show you what's happening:

  • Slack — a message for every payment and every deposit, saying what was done. This is the day-to-day view.
  • Executions in this panel — every run of every automation, newest first. Click one to expand it and see each step, what went in and what came back.

A run is marked success, skipped (nothing needed doing), review (waiting for a person) or error. Errored runs have a Re-run button: once whatever caused the failure is fixed, press it and the automation runs again with the same information. The original failure stays in the list, so the history is honest.
